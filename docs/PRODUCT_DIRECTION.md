# Crunch Week product direction

**The student promise: a realistic next step, even when study competes with work and training.**

Students supply confirmed deadlines, rough remaining effort and time they are genuinely free. Crunch Week turns those into sessions, records progress, and shows exactly what can or cannot fit. When plans change, recovery offers concrete choices with a visible time cost. The value depends on honest input and regular use; the planner cannot guarantee grades or prevent missed deadlines.

## The student workflow

1. Set study hours and protect weekly lectures, work and training, plus one-off shifts or appointments.
2. Add an assessment manually, or import a handbook and verify its source. Set a rough remaining-work estimate.
3. Open Today to pick a session, see commitments and urgent deadlines, and tick assignment steps.
4. Log completed study or move a missed session. Use On track / Need more time / Finished to keep the remaining estimate honest. Personal suggestions become available after enough completed work is logged.
5. Use scheduling-only chat for a work/training or effort change, or ask how an app feature works. Common help is local; a persistent 25-message daily allowance keeps this local installation bounded. For a change, review editable details and scheduling effects, then confirm. Direct app edits take priority. When work cannot fit, compare recovery choices, review the exact extra dates, and apply or undo a change.

This version is device-local. It supports backups and calendar snapshots; it has no accounts, shared staff dashboard, live LMS connection, hosted billing or cloud sync.

## Positioning to test

Syllabus imports and calendar planning already exist in products such as [Shovel](https://shovelapp.io/). [MyStudyLife](https://mystudylife.com/tour/) covers common student calendar and revision needs. Brightspace already offers an [upcoming/overdue Work To Do widget](https://community.d2l.com/brightspace/kb/articles/5174-about-the-work-to-do-widget). A generic list of deadlines alone is a weak reason to switch.

Crunch Week should test whether students value seeing a feasible plan around paid work and training, plus transparent recovery when time runs out. This is a positioning hypothesis, not evidence of an exclusive feature or market demand.

## Test demand before selling

Recruit a small student pilot with real commitments and verified assessment dates. Observe setup rather than teaching every click. Over two weeks, record time to the first valid plan, how often students return without prompting, whether logged sessions reflect work actually done, and whether recovery choices change what they do next. Ask which tool the app would replace and whether anyone would pay. Do not invent adoption statistics or infer academic failure from an estimated shortfall.

If students retain it, prioritize accounts and secure cross-device sync, reliable reminders, and approved timetable/LMS ingestion. The implemented conversational entry helper proposes editable dates/times and remaining hours for review, asks about ambiguity, and uses the deterministic planner to show scheduling effects. Test whether students understand the preview and can correct it without help; provider availability and interpretation quality still need real-world validation.

A university purchase would need a separate, permission-based product case: proven student benefit, accessibility, privacy controls, data retention rules, support, approved integrations and opt-in aggregate workload visibility. The current local app is a prototype for that case, not a deployable institutional service.
